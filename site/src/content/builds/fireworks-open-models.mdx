<ShortVersion>
Two open-weight models, **Kimi K3.1 Pro** and **DeepSeek V4.1 Flash**, served by Fireworks AI
from the US, are the first open models in Jigg.AI's process. Their first job was reviewing a
growth proposal for [MatSignal](/builds/matsignal/), after Claude, Grok and Gemini had. Setup took
an evening, and **a review pass cost 9 to 67 cents in inference** at Fireworks' list prices. In
this one round, both models independently caught something none of the other three reviewers had:
the proposal would have reversed a decision already on record. Worth it, as an extra critique
from outside the usual labs, with a human deciding what each finding is worth. The caveats: **nothing they found has been acted on yet**, and this is one review
round. It's the first step toward the AI-harness build that comes next.
</ShortVersion>

## Why reviewers from other labs

Every build in this journal so far was made by one AI tool under one person's direction. That's
the weak point of a one-person studio: there's no second engineer to say *that's wrong*. Since
MatSignal, its plans and specs have gone out for independent review to models from other labs,
Grok, Gemini and ChatGPT, on the theory that a model is worst at catching its own kind of
mistake.

That isn't a second accountable engineer. It's an independent critique channel that defends
against one model's blind spots, with a human adjudicating every finding, and it doesn't replace
expert review of a high-risk change, such as a security review. This round reviewed a growth
proposal, not production code.

Those are all closed models from large US labs. Kimi (from Moonshot AI) and DeepSeek are
open-weight models from Chinese labs, built by different teams, and other providers can host
them. Adding
them tests two things: whether reviewers that different add findings the others miss, and whether
they can run under the same rules as the rest, with their data handled the way the provider's
terms say.

## How it's set up

- **Fireworks runs both models** on its own servers, not the labs'. Both go through its US-only
  routes, which Fireworks describes as serving inference "exclusively from the US", for a
  premium: 1.25× the base price for Kimi and 1.5× for DeepSeek.
- **A small worker, built with Claude Code, gives each model a seat.** Each review runs on a fresh
  copy of the product's repository holding only tracked files, with read-only tools: list, read
  and search files, and see recent changes. No shell, no git, no writing, no network of its own.
- **Independence is enforced, not requested.** On a first pass the worker hides every earlier
  review from the model, and it can't read the issue where the others posted. The second pass
  gets the issue, so each model can answer the others by name.
- **Web access is narrow.** DeepSeek's seat can search through the Brave Search API and open only
  pages its search or its reading turned up. It honours each site's robots.txt and refuses the sites I've
  excluded, through redirects and archive copies too. Kimi's seat had no web access in this round.
- **It can't cite what it didn't read.** A check refuses any source the run never opened.
- **Money has a cap.** Before every call, the worst case has to fit what's left of a per-run cap,
  and a run stopped by its cap can resume. The cap is mine to set, in chat, with an amount.
- **Assignments arrive through Slack**, and each review comes back as a commit, posted unedited
  in the issue with its source link.

The architecture around this, approvals, budgets and lanes, is the subject of the next build,
the harness. This one is about getting two open models to do real work under these rules.

## The first round: MatSignal's growth proposal

The proposal was a plan for growing MatSignal without much of my time. Grok, Claude and Gemini
had already reviewed it in two passes each. On November 20 and 21, Kimi and DeepSeek each did a
first pass blind and a second pass with everything else visible.

**What only they found.** Both caught, independently and on their first pass, that the proposal's
automatic removal of thin pages from search contradicted a founder decision from November 17 that
every competition page is indexed. None of the three earlier reviewers had raised it. Beyond that:

- DeepSeek showed that a promise in the proposal, never to expose minors, couldn't be kept,
  because MatSignal stores no ages; that the proposal had dropped the success bar MatSignal had
  already set itself; and that a setting I use to keep my own visits out of the counts lapses
  after about a week in Safari.
- Kimi showed that hand-written competition pages skip the rule that a page needs at least one
  entry. DeepSeek's second pass found the other half: those pages also never leave the sitemap,
  so two of them were advertising a finished event from November 23 on.

DeepSeek's citations of MatSignal's code, file and line, checked out when they were spot-checked
afterwards.

**Where they were wrong, or changed their minds.** DeepSeek corrected two of its own first-pass
claims in its second pass, one of them about when pages were indexed, where Claude had it right.
Kimi withdrew one idea and demoted two others once it saw the other reviewers' evidence. DeepSeek credited a
point to the wrong numbered finding in Kimi's review. And when a cap note stopped Kimi
mid-review, Kimi blamed the cost cap; the cause was the worker's note, and the mismatch was caught
before anything was posted.

**What happened to it.** Nothing yet. The founder decisions on the proposal are still open, so
no finding from Kimi or DeepSeek has changed MatSignal so far. That's the number that will
matter, and it's carried forward.

## The honest process

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="Kimi on Bedrock: every call refused" tag="my-setup">
The plan was Kimi on Amazon Bedrock. Four calls in a row came back 401. The cause was mine:
macOS's password prompt had cut the API key to 128 characters when I read it from the keychain.
About 30 minutes lost. Then I switched to Fireworks, because it serves DeepSeek as well.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="The cost meter stopped Kimi after three calls, twice" tag="my-setup">
The worker's worst-case estimate counted every byte of a long file as a token, so a $1 cap looked
spent after three calls ($0.26, then $0.28). It now plans from the token counts Fireworks reports.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="It also charged the first review twice its list price" tag="my-setup">
Each call was charged at the higher of two readings of its usage. Kimi's first pass was metered at
$1.43; re-priced from its recorded usage, it cost $0.67. Fixed the same evening, and the
earlier figure stays in the record as charged.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="A last-call warning cut Kimi's second pass short" tag="my-setup">
The note that tells a model its next call is its last one treated 115 KB of reading as that many
tokens; the real count was about 27,000. Kimi stopped at $0.13 with an incomplete review, which
was not posted. A rerun finished for $0.31. Part of this is still open: a $1 cap can't cover
reading about 120,000 characters, and the worker has no tokenizer yet.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="A JSON schema came back as plain JSON mode" tag="tool-limit">
In the first hand test, a request for a strict JSON schema was echoed back by Fireworks as plain
JSON mode. The answer was valid JSON anyway, but strict schema enforcement is unconfirmed.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="The US routes aren't in the model list" tag="tool-limit">
Fireworks' list of available models doesn't show the US-only routes. They work when called by
name, which is how the worker calls them.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="fail" title="DeepSeek cited pages it had never opened" tag="tool-limit">
In a research test before the MatSignal review, DeepSeek cited pages it hadn't opened, and the
worker's citation check refused them. That's what the check is for.
</ProcessStep>

<ProcessStep result="pass" title="What worked">
Each model passed a hand test before any real work: Kimi found all four bugs planted in a sample,
7 calls for 4 cents, and DeepSeek's tool calls and reasoning worked in 3 calls for under a cent.
Prompt caching needed no setup: in Kimi's test, 4,307 of 4,308 repeated tokens came from the
cache, 83% cheaper. From choosing Fireworks to Kimi's first posted review took about four hours.
</ProcessStep>

and was fixed, and 1 is open. The tiles show eight of them; the test record has all 11.

## What went to Fireworks

Each review sent Fireworks a set of MatSignal's tracked documents and code (23 files for Kimi's
first pass), the issue's comments on a second pass, and the text of any page DeepSeek opened.
Both secret scans of the repository came back clean before its copy was made, and no list of
athletes was among the files read.

The worker calls one Fireworks API, Chat Completions. For open-model requests there, Fireworks'
terms say prompts and outputs aren't stored without opting in, the data is held "only in volatile
memory", and nothing trains on it without explicit opt-in; usage metadata is still logged. Its
other APIs differ: the Responses API stores data when `store` is on, its default, and the Batch
API isn't zero-retention. The worker uses neither. A secret scan isn't a full confidentiality
review either, and none of this clears client code to be sent anywhere: on client work, which
providers see the code is agreed with the client first. Two more limits, said plainly: the US-only routing is a product feature, not a promise in Fireworks' terms of service,
and prompt caches last "several minutes" by Fireworks' current account, where my notes from
November 20 said hours.

November 20 to the evening of November 21.

## What happens next

The next build is an experiment of its own: the AI harness this worker belongs to, with
assignments, approvals, budgets and lanes for every model, open or not. It's where findings from
rounds like this one get consolidated and counted: how many were verified, accepted and
implemented, and how many were false alarms. The number that matters is how often a review from
another lab changes what gets built.

## The tool

<ToolVerdict tool="Fireworks AI" toolSummary="Serverless inference for open-weight models · pay per token">
Fireworks did what it says: it served Kimi K3.1 Pro and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash from the US, at the
prices it lists as far as the run records show (the bill isn't reconciled yet), behind one API
key, with no prompts or outputs kept by default on the API the worker uses. Prompt caching
worked without being asked, which matters when every review re-reads the same files. Every bug in
this log that cost money was in my own worker, not in Fireworks.

The rough edges are small but real. The US-only routes don't appear in its model list, a strict
JSON schema came back as plain JSON mode, and the US region is a product feature rather than a
contractual term, so anyone with a legal reason to keep data in one country should get that in
writing. For putting open-weight models to work alongside the big labs' models, at cents a pass,
it's an AI tool worth your time.
